Lead paint · method comparison

Lead paint test kits: swab screen vs lab confirmation

Most U.S. housing built before 1978 (“target housing”) may contain lead-based paint. You cannot tell by looking. This page compares consumer color-change swabs and mail-in sample kits, with EPA recognition caveats. It is not medical, legal, or real-estate advice.

Federal definition of lead-based paint in housing: paint with lead ≥ 1.0 mg/cm² or 0.5% by weight (5,000 ppm). A pink swab is not that measurement.

Sources: EPA Lead Test Kits; EPA real-estate disclosures.

EPA-recognized kits are a screen — with hard limits

EPA recognizes three lead test kits for use under the Renovation, Repair, and Painting (RRP) Rule: LeadCheck™ (Luxfer Magtech), D-Lead® (ESCA Tech), and a Commonwealth of Massachusetts kit. They meet the negative-response criterion only. EPA states that, to date, no kit has met both the negative- and positive-response criteria in 40 CFR 745.88(c).

  • Recognition applies when a trained professional (certified renovator, inspector, or risk assessor) uses the kit on surfaces EPA evaluated.
  • LeadCheck™ and D-Lead®: wood, ferrous metal, drywall, plaster. Massachusetts kit: drywall and plaster only.
  • A negative result, used that way, can support a finding that regulated lead-based paint is not present on that tested surface. A positive result, a kit on an unevaluated surface, or a homeowner swab is not a lead-based paint inspection.
  • You may still assume lead is present in pre-1978 paint and follow lead-safe work practices without testing.

Buying or selling pre-1978 housing? The HUD/EPA disclosure rule gives buyers a 10-day opportunity for a certified inspection or risk assessment. Do not treat a DIY swab as that inspection. Pamphlet: Protect Your Family From Lead in Your Home.

When to hire a certified inspector or risk assessor

  • Buying or selling pre-1978 housing and you want the disclosure-period inspection to mean something.
  • A child under 6, a pregnant person, or someone with a lead-related medical concern lives (or will live) there.
  • Paint is peeling or will be disturbed by renovation, and you need hazards mapped — not just a color change.
  • You need an inspection report (where LBP is), a risk assessment (current hazards), or both.
  • A kit is positive, inconclusive, or was used on a surface EPA did not evaluate.

Start with EPA — find a qualified lead professional or your state lead program. For NLLAP labs and transcribed Michigan inspector lists, see Shortell’s Lead Paint Index (separate site).
