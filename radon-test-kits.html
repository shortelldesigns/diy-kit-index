EPA action level: EPA recommends fixing a home if the radon level is 4 picocuries per liter (pCi/L) or higher. EPA also notes there is no known safe level and that Americans may consider reducing levels between 2 and 4 pCi/L. Average indoor U.S. level is about 1.3 pCi/L; outdoor air about 0.4 pCi/L.

Radon is a colorless, odorless radioactive gas. Testing is the only way to know the level in a specific building. This page compares two common DIY approaches and states when a certified measurement professional is the right tool.

1. Short-term charcoal (mail-in)

A charcoal canister or liquid-scintillation vial sits in the lowest lived-in level, typically 2–7 days (short-term devices can run up to 90 days depending on type). You mail it to the lab. Follow kit instructions: closed-house conditions, away from drafts, kitchens, and bathrooms.

EPA’s citizen pattern: take a short-term test; if it is 4 pCi/L or higher, follow up with a second short-term test or a long-term test before deciding to mitigate. One unconfirmed kit is a weak basis for a mitigation contract when you can retest.

2. Continuous radon monitor (CRM)

A CRM records radon over time, often hourly, for at least 48 hours in professional protocols. Certified measurement professionals use qualified CRMs. Consumer devices such as the Airthings Corentium Home are useful for screening and trend watching; they are not a substitute for a state-accepted professional protocol when a contract, lender, or post-mitigation verification requires one.

Closed-house conditions beat buying a second kit

Charcoal canisters and continuous monitors both fail the same way when windows stay open or HVAC runs oddly during the test window. EPA closed-house guidance exists so a high reading means something; a second kit does not fix a bad setup. Short-term vs. long-term radon tests: what each result represents

The charcoal kit and the consumer monitor above are both usually used for short-term readings. EPA separates tests by how long they stay in the home, because radon levels “vary from day to day and season to season” (EPA, A Citizen’s Guide to Radon).

Test placement and conditions: EPA do’s and don’ts

Whichever device you use, follow its instructions first. These points come from EPA’s consumer guides (Citizen’s Guide; Home Buyer’s and Seller’s Guide):

Do

  • Place the device in the lowest lived-in level: the basement if it’s used often, otherwise the first floor.
  • Pick a room people use regularly, such as a living room, playroom, den, or bedroom.
  • Set it at least 20 inches above the floor where it won’t be disturbed.
  • For a test of 2–3 days (the buyer’s guide says under four days), close windows and outside doors at least 12 hours before you start.
  • Keep windows and outside doors closed during the test, except for normal entry and exit.
  • Run heating and cooling normally. Recirculating HVAC fans may run.
  • Write down start and stop dates and times, then reseal and mail the kit to the lab right away.

Don’t

  • Don’t test in a kitchen, bathroom, laundry room, furnace room, closet, stairway, hallway, or crawl space.
  • Don’t place it near drafts, high heat, high humidity, or exterior walls.
  • Don’t run fans or machines that bring in outside air. Radon-system fans and short bursts from small exhaust fans are allowed.
  • Don’t run a 2–3 day test during severe storms or unusually high winds.
  • Don’t move or disturb the device. EPA notes that interfering with a test may invalidate it and is illegal in some states.

When DIY is the wrong tool — hire a certified tester

  • The home is under contract and the buyer, seller, or lender needs a professional result.
  • You need a report that will stand up in a real-estate addendum.
  • The building is large, unusual, multi-unit, or already has a mitigation system (post-mitigation test).
  • You cannot keep closed-house conditions or place/retrieve the device yourself.

EPA tells the public to start with the state radon program list, then the national proficiency programs EPA recognizes: NRPP and NRSB. Some states require their own certification to test for others; a national credential alone is not always enough.

Results close to 4 pCi/L are the least clear-cut. EPA gives the example that if two short-term tests average 4.1 pCi/L, there’s about a 50% chance your year-round average is somewhat below 4 (EPA Citizen’s Guide). On risk, EPA estimates radon causes about 21,000 lung cancer deaths a year in the U.S. and calls it the second leading cause of lung cancer (EPA — Health Risk of Radon). A test result doesn’t diagnose anyone. For health questions, talk with a health professional.

Retesting after mitigation, renovation, or a move downstairs

  • After a radon system goes in: EPA says to test again after the home is fixed, then retest every two years to make sure levels stay low. Most soil-suction systems include a monitor that shows whether the system is working (Citizen’s Guide).
  • Sealing alone isn’t a fix: EPA does not recommend sealing cracks as the only step. In most cases, a vent-pipe-and-fan (sub-slab depressurization) system is used (Home Buyer’s and Seller’s Guide).
  • Before and after finishing a basement: test the area before a major renovation, and test again after the work is done (Citizen’s Guide).
  • New homes: EPA says every new home should be tested after occupancy, even if it was built radon-resistant.
  • Private well: if the air test shows a problem and your water comes from a well, EPA suggests testing the water too. Point-of-use devices at a tap don’t address radon released into the air from water used throughout the house (Citizen’s Guide).

A consumer monitor like the Airthings Corentium Home can show trends between formal tests. The confirmation test after mitigation should follow your state’s requirements. For DIY mitigation, EPA says to contact your state radon office first, and lowering high levels is generally a job for a trained contractor.

Radon testing FAQ

Which homes should be tested?

EPA and the Surgeon General recommend testing all homes below the third floor. EPA adds that any home can have a radon problem: new or old, well-sealed or drafty, with or without a basement (EPA Citizen’s Guide).

My neighbor’s result was low. Do I still need to test?

Yes. EPA says radon problems may be more common in some areas, but the only way to know about your home is to test it.

Can two kits side by side help?

For home sales, EPA lists two short-term tests run at the same time in the same location for at least 48 hours as one acceptable option, and the average decides. Its consumer guide describes side-by-side tests placed four inches apart (Home Buyer’s and Seller’s Guide; Citizen’s Guide). Real-estate tests should also include steps to prevent or detect interference, so a qualified tester is usually the better route.
