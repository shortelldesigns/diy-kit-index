EPA action level: EPA recommends fixing a home if the radon level is 4 picocuries per liter (pCi/L) or higher. EPA also notes there is no known safe level and that Americans may consider reducing levels between 2 and 4 pCi/L. Average indoor U.S. level is about 1.3 pCi/L; outdoor air about 0.4 pCi/L.

Radon is a colorless, odorless radioactive gas. Testing is the only way to know the level in a specific building. This page compares two common DIY approaches and states when a certified measurement professional is the right tool.

Sources: EPA action level; Find a kit or professional. Guidance summarized for homeowners; confirm current state protocols before a transaction.

Two DIY methods, different jobs

What you get One lab-reported average for the exposure period (often 2–7 days) Digital short- and long-term averages; some hourly logging
Strength Low cost; lab analyzes the charcoal; good first look Shows variation; reusable; useful for screening trends

1. Short-term charcoal (mail-in)

A charcoal canister or liquid-scintillation vial sits in the lowest lived-in level, typically 2–7 days (short-term devices can run up to 90 days depending on type). You mail it to the lab. Follow kit instructions: closed-house conditions, away from drafts, kitchens, and bathrooms.

EPA’s citizen pattern: take a short-term test; if it is 4 pCi/L or higher, follow up with a second short-term test or a long-term test before deciding to mitigate. One unconfirmed kit is a weak basis for a mitigation contract when you can retest.

2. Continuous radon monitor (CRM)

A CRM records radon over time, often hourly, for at least 48 hours in professional protocols. Certified measurement professionals use qualified CRMs. Consumer devices such as the Airthings Corentium Home are useful for screening and trend watching; they are not a substitute for a state-accepted professional protocol when a contract, lender, or post-mitigation verification requires one.

Closed-house conditions beat buying a second kit

Charcoal canisters and continuous monitors both fail the same way when windows stay open or HVAC runs oddly during the test window. EPA closed-house guidance exists so a high reading means something; a second kit does not fix a bad setup. Read the how-to placement steps before you reorder — and treat any result that will affect a real-estate deal as a reason to hire a certified tester, not to stack more Amazon boxes.

When DIY is the wrong tool — hire a certified tester

  • The home is under contract and the buyer, seller, or lender needs a professional result.
  • You need a report that will stand up in a real-estate addendum.
  • The building is large, unusual, multi-unit, or already has a mitigation system (post-mitigation test).
  • You cannot keep closed-house conditions or place/retrieve the device yourself.

EPA tells the public to start with the state radon program list, then the national proficiency programs EPA recognizes: NRPP and NRSB. Some states require their own certification to test for others; a national credential alone is not always enough.

Official starting points: EPA — find a qualified provider; EPA — find a kit or professional. For state-compiled tester lists, see Shortell’s Radon Test Index (separate site).

After the number. Below 4 pCi/L: some risk remains; retest after renovations or every few years. At or above 4 pCi/L: confirm with follow-up, then hire a credentialed mitigator if the confirmed level stays high. Consumer booklet: EPA Consumer’s Guide to Radon Reduction.
