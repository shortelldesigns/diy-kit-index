Space heaters · winter

Space heater safety: tip-over and overheat shutoff, placement and plug checks

By Shortell Designs · Updated November 2026

Skip to the space heater searches

CPSC estimates that portable heaters, including electric space heaters, were involved in an average of 1,600 fires a year from 2020 to 2022, with an average of 70 deaths and 150 injuries a year. (CPSC, January 2026) NFPA reports that space heaters and heating stoves accounted for nearly a third (30%) of home heating equipment fires but 73% of the deaths in those fires (2020–2024 averages), and that 46% of heating equipment fires happen from December through February. (NFPA) This page covers electric heaters only: what to look for on the box, where the heater goes, and how to check the plug while it runs.

Before you buy: the label and the shutoffs

  • A safety certification mark. CPSC says to make sure a space heater has the seal of a nationally recognized testing laboratory such as UL. (CPSC OnSafety) A certified heater will have a safety certification mark. (CPSC Pub. 098) UL LLC and Intertek Testing Services, whose certification mark is the ETL Listed mark, are both on OSHA's list of Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratories. (OSHA NRTL list) (OSHA: Intertek)
  • The standard behind it. The voluntary safety standard for portable electric room heaters is UL 1278, Standard for Movable and Wall- or Ceiling-Hung Electric Room Heaters (4th edition), which CPSC staff work on with UL. (CPSC: Electric Heaters)
  • Tip-over protection. UL says listed portable electric heaters must pass a tip-over test that simulates the worst tip-over position, and the heater must not start a fire on the terry cloth it is tipped onto. Makers can pass that test with temperature-limiting controls or a tip-over switch. If a switch is used, it must cut power before the heater tips past its balance point. (UL, The Code Authority, 2009) So "UL listed" alone does not guarantee a switch. If you want one, look for "tip-over switch" or "tip-over shutoff" in the product's own description.
  • Thermostat and automatic shutoff. CPSC advises using an electric heater with thermostat control and an automatic device that turns the heater off if it tips over frontwards or backwards. (CPSC) Los Angeles County's 2026 space heater bulletin says a heater should have a thermostat that shuts off when the set temperature is reached, and an automatic tip-over shutdown that turns the unit off if it is knocked over. A heater without that tip-over shutdown must have overheat protection that shuts the unit off if it overheats. (L.A. County, Feb 2026)
  • Recalls. Before you use any household product, check cpsc.gov/recalls. If yours is recalled, stop using it and contact the recalling company for the remedy. (CPSC, January 2026)

Placement checklist

  • Keep the heater at least three feet from drapes, furniture, bedding and other things that can burn. (CPSC, January 2026) CPSC measures that three feet from the front, sides and rear of the heater. (CPSC Pub. 098)
  • Set it on a stable, level surface where it will not be knocked over. (CPSC Pub. 098) Keep it out of traffic. (CPSC)
  • Never run the cord under rugs or carpeting; that can damage the cord and make it, and things near it, burn. (CPSC Pub. 098)
  • Keep electric heaters away from water, and never touch one if you are wet. (CPSC Pub. 098) In a bathroom, never place the heater near the tub or sink where it could fall into water. (CPSC)
  • Supervise young children in a room with a space heater, and teach them never to put fingers or objects through the guard. (CPSC)
  • Turn heaters off when you sleep. (CPSC, January 2026) Turn them off when you leave the room, too. (U.S. Fire Administration)

Plug and outlet checklist

  • Plug the heater directly into a wall outlet, never into an extension cord or power strip. (CPSC, January 2026)
  • The plug should fit tightly in the outlet. If it doesn't, don't use that outlet for the heater. (CPSC Pub. 098)
  • Before use, inspect the heater, cord and plug for damage, and never run a heater you suspect is damaged. (CPSC Pub. 098)
  • While it runs, check often whether the plug, cord, wall outlet or faceplate is hot. If the plug, outlet or faceplate is hot, stop using the heater and have a qualified electrician check and/or replace the plug or faulty wall outlet. If the cord is hot, unplug the heater and have it inspected or repaired by an authorized repair person. (CPSC Pub. 098) CPSC technical staff have investigated heaters with thermally damaged plugs from different manufacturers and wrote to UL about it in 2022. (CPSC: Electric Heaters)

Smoke alarms, and where carbon monoxide fits

CPSC says to put smoke alarms on every level of the home and inside each bedroom. Put CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, with battery power or battery backup. Test them monthly. (CPSC, January 2026) CPSC ties carbon monoxide to fuel-burning equipment such as furnaces, fireplaces and generators, and warns never to refuel a fuel-burning portable heater while it is in use. (CPSC, January 2026) If any fuel-burning appliance heats your home, compare carbon monoxide alarms for heating season.

Power outage: a generator never comes inside

CPSC says gasoline-powered generators used in winter outages can produce as much CO as hundreds of cars, and estimates about 750 people died from CO poisoning associated with generators alone from 2012 to 2022. Use a portable generator outdoors only, at least 20 feet from the home with the exhaust pointed away from any nearby building, and never in a home, basement, shed or garage. CPSC also recommends battery flashlights or lanterns instead of candles. (CPSC, January 2026) See our winter power-outage kit: battery power, light and a weather radio.

Thawing a frozen pipe

The Red Cross lists a portable space heater, kept away from flammable materials, as one safe way to warm a frozen pipe, along with an electric heating pad, a hair dryer or towels soaked in hot water, and says never to use an open flame. (American Red Cross) The placement and plug rules above still apply. Prevention steps are in our frozen-pipe kit: pipe sleeves, heat cable, faucet covers and a freeze alert.
